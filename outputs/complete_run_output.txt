--- THEORETICAL SUMMARY: TRAINING VS. INFERENCE ---
1. Training: The phase where the model learns statistical parameters/weights from labeled data.
   Here, CountVectorizer learns the vocabulary dictionary and document frequencies (fit_transform),
   and MultinomialNB calculates prior and likelihood probabilities P(w|c).
2. Inference: The phase where the trained model processes new, unseen text using fixed parameters.
   CountVectorizer converts the new text using the previously fitted vocabulary (transform),
   and the classifier evaluates argmax_c P(c|text) to output the predicted class without updating weights.

--- THEORETICAL SUMMARY: WHY TEXT PREPROCESSING IS USEFUL ---
1. Dimensionality Reduction: Without lowercasing, 'Course', 'course', and 'COURSE'
   would be treated as three distinct vocabulary tokens, inflating the feature matrix.
2. Noise Elimination: Punctuation (e.g., '!!!', '???', '---') often carries little semantic
   discrimination in bag-of-words tasks and fragments token matches (e.g., 'help!' vs 'help').
3. Data Consistency: Normalizing redundant whitespace ensures uniform token splitting across
   heterogeneous raw sources (web pages, user form inputs, PDFs).
4. Improved Generalization: Cleaner tokens enhance statistical overlap between training and inference data.

--- THEORETICAL SUMMARY: SPECIAL TOKENS & SUBWORD TOKENIZATION ---
============================================================
1. Special Tokens:
   - [CLS] (Classification Token): Added at the beginning of input sequence.
     Its hidden state vector is traditionally pooled for sequence classification tasks.
   - [SEP] (Separator Token): Marks boundaries between sentences (e.g., question & context).
   - [PAD] / [MASK] / [UNK]: Used for batch alignment, masked language modeling,
     and out-of-vocabulary fallback.

2. Subword Tokenization (WordPiece / BPE / Unigram):
   - Overcomes Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) limitations by decomposing rare or inflected words
     into common morphological sub-units (e.g., 'playing' -> 'play' + '##ing').
   - Balances vocabulary compactness (typically 30,000-50,000 tokens) with representation power,
     preventing the vocabulary explosion of word-level models while preserving semantic
     meaning better than purely character-level models.

--- THEORETICAL SUMMARY: WORD COUNTS VS. SEMANTIC EMBEDDINGS ---
1. Word Counts / TF-IDF (Sparse Representations):
   - Treat words as orthogonal dimensions in a massive, sparse vocabulary space.
   - Lack semantic awareness: 'forgot password' and 'reset password' have 0 overlap
     on those specific differing words, yielding low lexical similarity despite near-identical intent.
   - Suffer from the curse of dimensionality and cannot capture synonyms or context.

2. Dense Semantic Embeddings (Sentence Transformers):
   - Encode text into continuous, low-dimensional vector space (e.g. 384 or 768 dimensions).
   - Capture deep semantic meaning, context, and intent learned via contrastive pretraining.
   - Sentences sharing meaning map to vectors pointing in almost the same direction
     (high cosine similarity near 1.0), even with completely different vocabularies.
